What You'll Find in This Category

These communities range from general casual conversation groups to interest-based friendship circles (bonding over shared hobbies) and city-specific social groups that occasionally organize in-person meetups. Expect casual daily conversation, introduction threads for new members, and shared-interest discussion that naturally builds friendship over time. Some groups run more like an open lounge, where anyone can drop in at any hour and join whatever conversation is already happening. Others are more structured, with a pinned welcome message, a rotating "topic of the day," or admins who post icebreaker questions to keep quieter groups active.

You'll also find a mix of group sizes. Smaller WhatsApp groups, often capped at a few hundred members, tend to feel more intimate — people recognize each other's names, inside jokes develop, and conversations carry over from one day to the next. Larger Telegram groups and channels can host thousands of members, and while they feel less personal at first glance, many use topic threads or sub-groups so that a large community still has small, close-knit pockets within it.

Common Topics Discussed

Members engage in general casual conversation, introduction and "get to know you" threads, shared hobby or interest discussion that brings people together, local meetup coordination for city-specific groups, and general social support during day-to-day life. On any given day you might see someone asking for restaurant recommendations in their neighborhood, a member sharing a small win from their week, a poll about weekend plans, or a longer thread where people talk through something they're going through and others chime in with encouragement.

Many groups also lean into lighter, recurring formats to keep things active: "two truths and a lie" style introductions for newcomers, weekly check-ins, voice-note exchanges for members who prefer talking over typing, and the occasional group video call for those who want to put faces to names. The tone is usually casual and low-stakes — nobody is expected to have deep, meaningful conversations every day, and plenty of members simply enjoy having a place to say good morning to people who'll say it back.

Why People Join These Groups

Making new friends as an adult is notoriously harder than in school or college, since natural social opportunities decrease significantly, and these groups provide a structured way to meet new people with at least one shared context (the group's specific focus). For people who've relocated to a new city, these communities can also provide an initial social network before building deeper offline connections.

The reasons people join vary a lot from person to person. A few common ones:

  • New to a city or country: relocating for work or study often means starting a social life from zero, and a group gives an easy first foothold.
  • Remote or hybrid work: without a daily office, casual social contact has to be found on purpose rather than happening by default.
  • Life transitions: a breakup, a friend group drifting apart, or simply outgrowing old social circles can all prompt someone to look for new people.
  • Curiosity and low commitment: some people join just to see what it's like — there's no cost to lurking for a while before jumping into conversation.
  • Wanting a specific kind of connection: some are looking for a wide, loose social circle; others want one or two close friends and use the group as a starting point.

Whatever the motivation, most members find that the group works best when they treat it as a starting point rather than the whole relationship — the real friendship tends to form once conversation moves from the group into a smaller, more personal chat.

How to Get the Most Out of a Group Like This

Joining a group is easy; actually forming a connection takes a little more intention. A short introduction when you join — your name, a bit about where you're from or what you're into, maybe what you're hoping to get out of the group — tends to get a much warmer response than silently lurking for weeks. From there, responding to other people's messages, not just posting your own, is usually what turns "member of a group" into "person people recognize."

It also helps to find the one or two people whose messages you consistently enjoy reading, and reach out to them directly rather than waiting for a deeper connection to form in a group of hundreds. Most lasting friendships that start in these communities move to a one-on-one chat fairly quickly — the group is the introduction, not the whole relationship.

Safety When Building New Friendships Online

As with any online social space, apply reasonable caution when transitioning from group chat to one-on-one conversation, especially before meeting in person — video calls first, public meeting locations, and telling someone else your plans are sensible precautions. Be skeptical of anyone in the group asking for money or personal financial information, regardless of how genuine the friendship feels.

A few other habits are worth building early: avoid sharing your exact home address or daily routine with someone you've only just met online, be cautious of anyone who pushes hard to move off the platform and onto a less moderated app right away, and trust your instincts if a conversation starts to feel off, even if you can't immediately explain why. Most people in these groups are exactly who they say they are and simply want the same thing you do — genuine, low-pressure company — but a little caution costs nothing and protects the good experiences from the rare bad one.

Finding Your Specific Community

General social groups can feel less engaging than interest-based ones, since shared context tends to build friendship faster than pure random conversation. If you have a specific hobby or interest, our other category pages — from Books & Literature to Gaming & Esports to Fitness & Bodybuilding — often make better starting points for genuine friendship-building around something you're already passionate about. That said, general social groups still have a real place: they're a good fit if you're not sure yet what you're looking for, if you enjoy variety in who you talk to, or if you simply want a broader, more relaxed space without the built-in agenda of an interest group.

If a general group doesn't quite click, it's worth browsing two or three others rather than judging the category by a single group. Activity levels, admin style, and the personality of the existing members can vary a lot even between groups with an almost identical description.
